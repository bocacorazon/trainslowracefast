## Sample 1 — How I learned volume beats intensity — 2026-04-30

Context: founder origin story. Personal-narrative voice. ~280 words. Source draft:
`docs/voice.md`. Cleaned 2026-04-30: pronoun consistency (paragraph 2 unified to
first-person singular); *80/20 Running* italicized and author named.

When I discovered cycling, it did not take long to realize I had talent somewhat
above average. I liked to test it on every ride. I liked to push the pace, push
myself, end up feeling like I was going to collapse. I made very quick progress.
But at one point I realized I was not catching up to the fast guys. There was
always this moment on a very hard ride when it became a bit harder, and I did
not have that extra bit. I was at my limit. I also started to notice how tired
I always was, and that hopping on the bike seemed like a chore. My indoor
training sessions were exhausting and very mentally draining.

Then I came across a book, *80/20 Running* by Matt Fitzgerald, where he says
that the only training variable that showed significant, general correlation
with increased performance was volume. Not intensity, not the nature of the
training, but pure volume. And the big realization after that: you can have
intensity or you can have volume. Not both. That practical recipe hides the
actual mechanism by which the magic happens: increased mitochondria, blah,
blah, blah. You might care or you might not. I can point you to the sources
if you do. But the truth of the matter is that if you want to improve you
must run, ride, ski, row A LOT. And in order to run, ride, ski, or row a lot
you must not be tired, you must not be injured. A hard week will take more
than it gives, if not done with judgement. That's what I'm building this for.

**Voice characteristics observed in this sample:**
- Personal-narrative opening; willing to admit early-career arrogance.
- Negation rhythm ("Not intensity, not the nature of the training, but pure volume").
- Dismissive shorthand for unnecessary detail ("blah, blah, blah").
- Reader-respecting offer ("You might care or you might not").
- Conversational compound clauses, sparingly punctuated.
- Drops into "you" (second person) in the practical claim section.
