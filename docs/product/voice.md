When I discovered cycling, it did not take long to realize I had talent somewhat above average. I liked to test it on every ride. I liked to push the pace, push myself, end up feeling like I was going to collapse. I made very quick progress. But at one point I realized I was not catching up to the fast guys. There was always this moment on a very hard ride when it became a bit harder, and I did not have that extra bit. I was at my limit. I also started to notice how tired I always was, and that hopping on the bike seemed like a chore. My indoor training sessions were exhausting and very mentally draining.
Then I came across a book, 80/20 running, where he says that the only training variable that showed significant, general correlation with increased performance was volume. Not intensity, not the nature of the training, but pure volume. And the big realization after that: you can have intensity or you can have volume. Not both. That practical recipe hides the actual mechanism by which the magic happens: increased mitochondria, blah, blah, blah. You might care or you might not. We can point you to the sources if you are. But the truth of the matter is that if you want to improve you must run, ride, ski, row A LOT. And in order to run, ride, ski or row a lot you must not be tired, you must not be injured. A hard week will take more than it gives, if not done with judgement. We can help you with those.

If you have trained for a marathon, you have probably felt this. You are eight weeks out, doing a long run on the weekend, trying to stay disciplined and keep it in Z2. Fifteen miles later you are tired, a bit depleted, maybe it was hot or windy, and you know the run was not trivial. You stop the watch, save the activity, and Garmin gives you a shrug: no anaerobic benefit, modest aerobic stimulus, a training effect that feels almost insulting. Then a few days later you do a short hard run and the watch can barely contain its excitement. That is the trap. The device is not measuring the same thing your plan is trying to build. It is very good at noticing intensity. It is much less good at respecting restraint.

Cyclists know a version of the same story. You spend Saturday doing the right ride: six steady hours at around 50% of FTP, eating, drinking, keeping your ego under control. TrainingPeaks calls it 150 TSS. Then on Sunday you roll out for what was supposed to be an easy recovery spin, except the group has that dangerous mix of fitness and insecurity and suddenly you are attacking, closing gaps, and hanging on for dear life. Ninety minutes later you get home and the ride lands at 130 or 140 TSS.

One of those rides moved you toward your goal. The other probably stole from next week to flatter you today. Almost the same score. Very different consequences.

The problem is not that TSS and Training Effect are useless. The problem is that athletes mistake a partial measure for a complete one. These metrics are good at seeing intensity. They are much worse at seeing restraint, durability, and the real cost of long aerobic work. And those are often the things that decide whether an endurance block succeeds.

The other day I came across a set of race photos from a long gravel event, the race I still remember as the day I felt strongest in my entire racing life. In the first photo I am on the front of a break on a climb, doing my turn, smiling, relaxed. Then the sequence continues and you can see the faces behind me: five riders straining just to stay attached. I did not look like that because I had discovered some perfect interval set. I looked like that because, almost by accident, I had finally stopped trying to prove my fitness every week.

I had come into gravel burned out from road racing. I was tired, stale, and not especially excited to go hard anymore. So I started riding easier, mostly because it was all I felt like doing. Around the same time I found Matt Fitzgerald's *80/20 Running* and borrowed the idea for cycling. I started riding longer, with very little intensity, and hoped that if I ever decided to race hard again I would at least not have lost everything. When I showed up to that first gravel event, I went mostly to have fun. I genuinely did not know what I would have in my legs. To my surprise, I had plenty.

The strange part is that this lesson never stays learned. Later, when I decided to try triathlon, easy days started to feel like cheating again. Even with that gravel season behind me, I could still feel the old suspicion that I was not doing enough, not hurting enough, not earning the result I wanted. Athletes are wired to trust fatigue more than process. Suffering feels honest. Restraint feels lazy.

But progress is not measured by pain. Endurance training often works in ways that are emotionally unsatisfying at first. The sessions that make you feel the most serious are not always the sessions that make you better. Sometimes the real breakthrough is that you stop needing every workout to convince you it counted.
